# The Matrimonial Order: An Exhaustive Guide

## A Christian study of covenant, mutual responsibility, and Ephesians 5:21–33

**Dr. Coach Achu Gustave**

This guide takes Ephesians 5:21–33 as its central text and treats the passage as a demanding account of covenant responsibility. It reads the wife’s submission and the husband’s headship within the larger call to walk in love, honor Christ, and give oneself for another. The comparison with Christ and the church is serious, but it does not make a husband divine, sinless, or entitled to control his wife. Christ’s unique authority and saving work belong to Christ alone.

The chapters are written for individual study, couples, and facilitators. They offer a theological reading and practical exercises, not a substitute for pastoral care, medical treatment, legal advice, or professional counseling.

## Chapter 1: Begin with the Whole Passage

Marriage teaching is easily distorted when one sentence is detached from the passage around it. Ephesians 5:21–33 belongs to a letter about life in Christ: unity, transformed conduct, truthfulness, forgiveness, love, and the church’s common life. The marriage passage is not an isolated permission slip for one spouse to rule the other. It is an account of how a Christian household is to be shaped by allegiance to Christ.

Verse 21 introduces the household instructions with the call to submit to one another in reverence for Christ. In verses 22–24, wives are addressed directly. In verses 25–30, husbands are addressed directly and at greater length, with Christ’s self-giving love as their measure. Verse 31 recalls Genesis 2:24 and the one-flesh union; verse 32 identifies the mystery as profound; verse 33 summarizes the call to love and respect.

The passage gives distinct responsibilities, but distinct responsibilities do not mean unequal human worth. Both spouses are image-bearers and accountable to God. Both must speak truth, keep covenant, and seek the other’s good. The roles described here are not licenses to humiliate, threaten, isolate, assault, or silence.

Read the passage aloud twice. On the first reading, mark every command. On the second, mark every comparison and every stated purpose. Notice that the husband is told to love, give himself, nourish, and cherish; the wife is told to submit to her own husband in the Lord. Notice also that the whole instruction is governed by reverence for Christ.

The central question is not, “How can I make my spouse comply?” It is, “What obedience to Christ is being asked of me, and what would faithfulness look like in my conduct today?” That question keeps study from becoming a weapon.

**Study and practice**

1. Read Ephesians 5:15–33 and list the commands addressed to each spouse.
2. Which parts of the passage are easiest for you to quote? Which are easiest to neglect?
3. Write one sentence describing what reverence for Christ should change in your marriage this week.

## Chapter 2: What the Comparison Does—and Does Not—Say

The repeated word *as* links marriage conduct to the relationship of Christ and the church. The comparison matters: Paul is not describing a casual preference or a role that can be discarded whenever it is inconvenient. Yet a comparison is not an identity. A husband is not Christ, a wife is not the church in every respect, and marriage does not reproduce Christ’s saving authority.

The Greek word commonly translated *as* can introduce comparison or manner. The word by itself does not establish that husband and wife possess identical authority, nor does it settle every question about the degree or limits of submission. Christians have interpreted the structure of this passage in different ways. This guide takes its commands seriously while avoiding claims that a single adverb proves more than the passage itself says.

“As unto the Lord” places a wife’s conduct within her relationship with Christ. Her faithfulness is not worship of her husband. Her husband is not divine authority over her; he is a human spouse who remains accountable to God. A wife may honor her husband without treating his judgment as infallible. A husband may receive respect without demanding reverence that belongs to God.

The comparison also places an extraordinarily high burden on a husband. The standard named is Christ’s self-giving love. He is not told to use the church’s submission to secure his preferences. He is told to give himself, serve, nourish, and cherish. If a man invokes Christ’s headship to excuse selfishness, intimidation, or domination, he has contradicted the very example he cites.

A careful reader holds both parts together: the text assigns responsibilities, and those responsibilities are bounded by Christ’s character and Christian moral duties. Where a claim about “the order” produces pride, fear, concealment, or injury, the claim should be examined against the passage’s commands to love and give oneself.

**Study and practice**

1. Explain the difference between a comparison and an identity.
2. What would change if a husband treated Christ’s self-giving as the measure of his leadership?
3. Discuss which interpretations of “as” you have encountered and what evidence each interpretation uses.

## Chapter 3: “As Unto the Lord”

The phrase “as unto the Lord” gives Christian submission its spiritual orientation. A wife’s conduct is part of her discipleship. It is not a declaration that her husband is God, that he is always right, or that she must surrender her conscience. The Christian’s first allegiance is to the Lord.

That allegiance can make submission more serious, not less thoughtful. A wife can listen carefully, speak respectfully, support a decision she believes is wise, and choose cooperation rather than rivalry. She can also raise a concern, name a risk, ask for more time, or refuse a demand that would require sin or serious harm. Integrity is not rebellion merely because it is inconvenient to a spouse.

Submission is therefore neither contempt for the husband nor erasure of the wife. It is a posture of covenant faithfulness that should be visible in ordinary choices: keeping promises, refusing manipulation, sharing information honestly, welcoming correction, and seeking agreement without turning every difference into a contest for status.

The husband’s corresponding responsibility is to make such trust reasonable. Trust is built by truthfulness, reliability, humility, and a willingness to hear “no” or “not yet” without punishment. A husband who wants his wife’s confidence must show himself worthy of confidence. He cannot command the inner reality of trust; he can cultivate it through faithful action.

Couples can distinguish a preference from a moral conviction. A preference may be yielded as an act of generosity. A conviction should be explained and respected. A serious safety concern calls for protection and qualified help, not a demand to submit more quietly. These distinctions help couples honor the text without confusing peace with silence.

**Study and practice**

1. Name one area where you can cooperate more freely and one area where you need an honest conversation.
2. Practice the sentence: “I want to understand you, and I also need to tell you what concerns me.”
3. Ask: What actions make respect and trust easier to give in our relationship?

## Chapter 4: The Wife’s Calling—Honor Without Disappearing

Ephesians addresses wives as moral agents. The command is not “become invisible,” “have no judgment,” or “accept every request.” The wife is a person addressed by Scripture, responsible for her choices before God. Her vocation in marriage is not fulfilled by silence but by faithful participation in a covenant shared with another accountable person.

Honor can be active. It includes refusing public contempt, speaking truth without cruelty, recognizing a husband’s efforts, and making room for his gifts. It can include offering counsel before a decision, standing with him in a difficult season, or taking initiative when he is exhausted or unable. A helper is not a decoration. Help involves strength, discernment, and action.

Honor is not flattery. It does not require pretending that a harmful choice is wise or that a broken promise has been kept. Honest correction, offered with care, can serve a marriage better than praise that conceals a serious problem. Likewise, a wife’s disagreement should not be framed automatically as disloyalty. A couple needs a way to hear disagreement before resentment becomes their only language.

The wife’s identity also exceeds her marital role. She remains a person with gifts, friendships, responsibilities, convictions, and a calling before God. Marriage should not require isolation from safe relationships or the loss of access to practical resources. A healthy covenant makes room for both spouses to mature and contribute.

This chapter invites wives to reflect without placing all responsibility on them. A wife can ask what she is bringing to the relationship, and a husband must ask the same. Covenant growth is not a project assigned to only one spouse.

**Study and practice**

1. Write down three ways you have experienced your spouse’s contribution to the marriage.
2. Identify one difficult truth that needs a respectful conversation.
3. Each spouse completes: “I feel honored when…” and “I feel dismissed when…”

## Chapter 5: “In Everything”—Comprehensive, Not Conscience-Free

Verse 24 says that wives are to be subject to their own husbands “in every thing.” The phrase expresses a comprehensive orientation to the marriage described in the passage. It should not be reduced to occasional cooperation only when the wife happens to agree. At the same time, no human relationship gives one person unlimited moral authority over another.

The New Testament repeatedly places human obedience under obedience to God. No spouse may require the other to lie, commit a crime, participate in abuse, abandon a child’s safety, or violate conscience. A marriage vow does not make wrong conduct right. Neither “in everything” nor any other biblical phrase should be used to forbid a person from seeking help or protection.

In ordinary life, many decisions are prudential rather than moral: schedules, purchases, hospitality, work transitions, or how to organize a room. Couples can make these decisions by gathering facts, hearing each person’s concerns, identifying who carries the greatest risk, and agreeing on a reversible next step. A household may choose a designated decision process, but the process should never excuse a spouse from listening or taking responsibility for consequences.

When spouses disagree, slow the conversation. Ask what is at stake, what each person fears losing, and what additional information would help. If a decision affects one spouse’s body, safety, livelihood, or vocation especially heavily, that spouse’s perspective deserves particular attention. “Headship” is not a shortcut around understanding.

The goal is not to win a theological argument. The goal is to act faithfully, tell the truth, and protect the covenant from needless injury. A couple that cannot resolve a recurring disagreement may benefit from a trusted pastor or qualified counselor who will hear both spouses and take safety concerns seriously.

**Study and practice**

1. Sort three current disagreements into preference, moral conviction, and safety concern.
2. Agree on a process for decisions that affect both spouses.
3. Practice asking, “What would make this decision faithful and safe for both of us?”

## Chapter 6: Headship Measured by Christ

The phrase “the husband is the head of the wife” has been understood in different ways across Christian traditions. In this guide, headship is read in the context Paul supplies: Christ’s relation to the church and the husband’s command to love as Christ loved. Whatever a couple’s precise theological account, headship cannot honestly be separated from costly responsibility.

Christ’s example is not self-protection at another’s expense. He gives himself. The husband is therefore called to put the good of his wife ahead of the convenience of his ego. He is to take responsibility rather than claim privilege; to serve rather than demand applause; to hear rather than silence; to protect rather than endanger; to repent rather than blame.

Leadership in a household is not measured by how quickly others comply. It is measured by whether the husband’s presence helps create truth, safety, fidelity, and shared flourishing. A husband who refuses accountability is not displaying strength. A husband who can admit harm, seek repair, and change course is demonstrating moral courage.

The image of Christ and the church is not permission to treat a wife like a subordinate employee. A wife is not a project to be managed. She is a covenant partner whose insight and gifts matter. The husband cannot claim to love his wife while disregarding her experience of the decisions he makes.

This standard is demanding because it removes easy excuses. A husband cannot point to the word *head* while ignoring the verbs *love*, *give*, *nourish*, and *cherish*. A wife should not have to suffer in order to prove that her husband is a leader. The husband’s own conduct is under judgment by the Lord he is called to imitate.

**Study and practice**

1. Husbands: name a recent instance when you chose service over self-interest.
2. Wives: describe specific behaviors that help you experience care and safety.
3. Together: define what accountability looks like when a decision has caused harm.

## Chapter 7: Love That Gives Itself

Ephesians 5:25 tells husbands to love their wives as Christ loved the church and gave himself for it. This is not a vague instruction to feel affection. It calls for durable action: attention, honesty, restraint, responsibility, and willingness to bear a cost for another’s good.

Self-giving love does not mean a husband must agree to every request or abandon sound judgment. It does mean he cannot make his comfort the unquestioned center of family life. It asks whether his time, money, work, habits, and emotional energy are being stewarded for the good of the covenant, not merely for his own wishes.

Love also refuses coercion. A gift demanded under threat is not a gift. A spouse who uses Scripture to frighten, isolate, or control the other is not demonstrating Christlike love. The cross cannot be used to tell the wounded person to accept more injury while the person causing harm avoids repentance.

Practical love is often ordinary: keeping an agreement, sharing care work, arranging time to listen, taking responsibility for a mistake, honoring a boundary, and doing a task without requiring praise. These habits are not beneath spiritual leadership. They are where its credibility is tested.

Husbands should not wait for their wives to become perfectly lovable before acting in love. Wives should not be asked to carry the entire emotional and spiritual life of the marriage. Both spouses owe one another truth and good faith; the husband’s particular instruction here is to love with a self-giving pattern.

**Study and practice**

1. List three costly, concrete acts of love your spouse would recognize.
2. Ask your spouse which of these actions would help most in the coming week.
3. Choose one action and complete it without turning it into a debt.

## Chapter 8: Nourish and Cherish

Paul describes a husband as nourishing and cherishing his own body, then applies this image to the church and to marriage. The picture is one of care, not ownership. A husband who loves his wife does not treat her wellbeing as an inconvenience separate from his own. He takes seriously how his choices affect her body, mind, faith, and daily life.

To nourish is to provide what supports growth: dependable care, truthful words, practical help, encouragement, and attention to needs. To cherish is to treat someone as precious: to listen, protect privacy, show tenderness, and avoid contempt. Neither word excuses infantilizing a wife or making decisions about her without her participation.

The body language in this passage should lead spouses toward consideration, not entitlement. Marriage does not eliminate bodily autonomy or the need for consent. Sexual intimacy is not owed under threat. Each spouse’s body, health, fatigue, and expressed boundaries matter. A couple can speak openly about desire, timing, medical needs, and forms of affection without shame or pressure.

Care is reciprocal in the wider life of marriage, even as the passage speaks directly to the husband’s duty. Wives also nourish and cherish. Both partners can notice when the other is depleted, ask what support is welcome, and make room for rest and restoration. Care should be practical enough to be felt, not just promised.

**Study and practice**

1. Ask: “What helps you feel cared for when life is demanding?”
2. Identify one recurring task or burden that could be shared more fairly.
3. Agree on a simple signal for when either spouse needs rest or a pause.

## Chapter 9: One Flesh in Daily Life

Ephesians 5:31 quotes Genesis: a man leaves father and mother, is joined to his wife, and the two become one flesh. One flesh is more than romance. It names a covenant union that changes loyalties and creates a shared life while preserving two distinct persons.

Leaving does not require contempt for parents or family of origin. It means the marriage becomes a primary household bond. Couples may need loving boundaries around relatives, finances, private disagreements, and parenting decisions. Those boundaries are strongest when discussed together rather than imposed secretly by one spouse.

Clinging means sustained fidelity. It is practiced through reliability, truthfulness, affection, and the repair of trust after ordinary failures. Shared life includes calendars, money, chores, caregiving, grief, celebration, and plans for the future. If one spouse carries nearly every invisible task, the couple should name the imbalance and make a workable adjustment.

Unity is not uniformity. Spouses may have different temperaments, interests, and friendships. They can pursue individual growth while remaining faithful to the covenant. The point is not to erase difference but to govern difference with loyalty and care.

**Study and practice**

1. What boundary with family or work would help protect the marriage?
2. List the shared tasks that keep your home functioning. Who currently carries each?
3. Choose one way to express fidelity and one way to express friendship this week.

## Chapter 10: Decisions and Shared Household Stewardship

Ephesians describes a moral order, not a detailed manual for every budget meeting or household decision. Couples therefore need habits that translate principle into practice. A dependable process is more faithful than relying on stereotypes, guessing, or the loudest voice in the room.

Begin with shared information. Make important facts visible: costs, time, health effects, obligations, and possible consequences. Give each spouse time to explain what matters. Identify whether the decision is reversible, urgent, or likely to affect one person more than the other. Decide together how to proceed, then set a date to review the result.

Some couples will understand final responsibility differently because of their theological tradition. Even then, responsibility does not eliminate consultation. A husband who believes he bears a particular leadership burden should be more willing to listen, not less. He should also be willing to own the outcome rather than blame his wife if his choice fails.

Decisions involving safety, sexual consent, a spouse’s medical care, legal rights, or a child’s protection require special care. No theological role can authorize force, concealment, or the denial of necessary care. Ask qualified professionals for help where expertise is needed.

**Study and practice**

1. Select one pending decision and list facts, values, risks, and options.
2. Each spouse states the strongest concern held by the other before recommending a plan.
3. Set a review date and agree how to revisit the decision if it is not working.

## Chapter 11: Speech, Conflict, and Repair

Christian marriage does not mean conflict disappears. Two people with histories, limits, and responsibilities will sometimes misunderstand one another. The question is whether conflict becomes a place for truth and repair or a routine of contempt, intimidation, and withdrawal.

Start with a description, not a verdict. “We missed the appointment we agreed to” is more useful than “You never care about us.” Speak from your own experience, ask clarifying questions, and listen long enough to summarize what you heard. A pause can help when either person is overwhelmed, but a pause should include a clear time to return rather than become indefinite avoidance.

Apology is specific. It names the action, acknowledges its effect, avoids excuses, and states what will change. Forgiveness is not pretending that trust has been restored; trust grows through repeated, observable reliability. The person harmed is not responsible for making the apology feel successful.

Some conflict cannot be safely handled in private. Threats, stalking, physical or sexual violence, coercive control, or fear of retaliation call for safety planning and qualified support, not a communication exercise. A couples conversation may be unsafe when one spouse uses disclosures to punish the other.

**Study and practice**

1. Each spouse describes one recurring conflict without using “always” or “never.”
2. Practice reflecting the other person’s concern before responding.
3. If an apology is owed, make it specific and name one measurable change.

## Chapter 12: Intimacy, Affection, and Mutual Care

Marriage includes spiritual, emotional, practical, and physical closeness. These forms of intimacy are related, but they are not interchangeable. Sexual access cannot repair contempt by itself, and a busy household cannot thrive on logistics alone. Couples benefit from making room for affection, conversation, rest, and shared pleasure.

Sexual intimacy should be mutual, freely chosen, and attentive to health and circumstance. A marriage vow is not ongoing consent to every act at every time. Either spouse may decline, pause, or seek medical advice. Pressure, punishment, or spiritual threats undermine the trust intimacy requires. A caring couple can talk about desire differences without labeling the lower-desire spouse as disloyal or defective.

Affection can be expressed in many ways: conversation, gentle touch that is welcome, shared rituals, play, acts of service, and words of appreciation. Ask rather than assume. What was welcome in one season may not be welcome in another because of illness, pregnancy, trauma, grief, medication, or changing needs.

For Christian couples, prayer and Scripture can deepen intimacy when shared freely. They should never be used to override a spouse’s honest limits. Spiritual practice is a source of mutual comfort, not a tool for extracting compliance.

**Study and practice**

1. Discuss what kinds of affection feel welcome and what kinds do not.
2. Plan one low-pressure time for connection that does not require a particular outcome.
3. Identify health questions that deserve a clinician’s guidance.

## Chapter 13: Safety, Abuse, and the Limits of Human Authority

No person should be told that faithfulness requires enduring violence or credible threats. Abuse may include physical or sexual assault, intimidation, stalking, isolation, financial control, threats involving children or immigration status, and repeated use of religious language to dominate. The harmed person is not responsible for causing another person’s abuse.

The language of submission, headship, forgiveness, or covenant must not be used to conceal danger or obstruct help. A spouse may seek support from trusted people, a qualified domestic-violence service, a clinician, law enforcement, or legal counsel. If there is immediate danger, contact local emergency services. Safety planning should be tailored with trained help; confronting an abusive partner or announcing plans to leave can increase risk.

Repentance requires more than regret or a promise to change. It includes taking responsibility, ending the harmful behavior, accepting appropriate consequences, respecting boundaries, and engaging qualified intervention. The harmed spouse does not owe immediate reconciliation, restored access, or secrecy.

Pastors and facilitators should listen without blaming, assess safety, and refer beyond their expertise. Couples counseling is not always appropriate when coercive control or violence is present. A professional experienced in abuse dynamics can help determine safer next steps.

These safeguards do not weaken Christian marriage. They affirm that people are accountable to God and that a spouse is not God. Protecting life and truth is compatible with honoring covenant; enabling harm is not.

**Study and practice**

1. Identify safe people and professional services you could contact if danger arose.
2. Facilitators: review local referral information before teaching this chapter.
3. Do not use this exercise to pressure a person to disclose danger in an unsafe setting.

## Chapter 14: Covenant Through Changing Seasons

Marriage is lived over time. Illness, disability, unemployment, infertility, pregnancy, grief, relocation, aging, and caregiving can change what each spouse is able to give. A role is not a reason to ignore reality. Faithfulness often requires adapting responsibilities so that both people remain cared for.

In a season of illness, the healthy spouse may carry more for a time, while receiving support from family, community, or professionals. During job loss, couples may need a temporary budget and honest discussion about shame or fear. When children arrive, spouses should protect the marriage relationship while sharing the work of care. When children leave, the couple can rediscover companionship and revise plans.

Change does not automatically mean failure. It asks spouses to communicate, grieve what has changed, and choose the next faithful step. The best plan is often modest: one appointment, one shared task, one honest conversation, one request for help.

**Study and practice**

1. Name the season you are in and one way it is affecting each spouse.
2. Ask what support from outside the marriage would be helpful and safe.
3. Choose one expectation that needs to be revised for this season.

## Chapter 15: A Thirty-Day Study and Practice Plan

Use this plan individually or as a couple. If studying together does not feel safe, study with a trusted facilitator instead. Keep a journal of observations and commitments; do not use a spouse’s private reflections as ammunition in a later disagreement.

**Week 1 — Read faithfully**

- Day 1: Read Ephesians 5:15–33. Note the surrounding instructions.
- Day 2: Read Genesis 2:18–25. Reflect on covenant and one flesh.
- Day 3: List the commands addressed to wives and husbands.
- Day 4: Identify assumptions you bring to the word *head*.
- Day 5: Identify assumptions you bring to the word *submit*.
- Day 6: Discuss what the comparison to Christ does and does not mean.
- Day 7: Rest, pray, or review the week’s notes.

**Week 2 — Practice honor and self-giving**

- Each day, notice one contribution your spouse makes.
- Choose two concrete acts of care and complete them freely.
- Have one conversation about a concern using clear, non-accusing language.
- Ask whether a household burden is being carried fairly.

**Week 3 — Build shared habits**

- Review one household decision process.
- Make one practical plan for finances, time, or caregiving.
- Set a regular check-in that both spouses can attend.
- Discuss boundaries with work, relatives, or technology.

**Week 4 — Repair and renew**

- Name one promise you can keep more consistently.
- Offer a specific apology if one is needed.
- Agree on one measurable practice to continue for the next month.
- Seek qualified help for recurring problems that exceed your skills.

**Closing reflection**

Each spouse completes these sentences privately, then shares only what feels safe:

- I understand this passage differently now because…
- One way I can honor Christ in my conduct is…
- One form of care I need is…
- One way I can make care easier to receive is…
- One question I still want to study is…

The aim is not a perfect household or a victory in debate. It is a more truthful, faithful, safe, and loving covenant life under the lordship of Christ.

## Chapter 16: Four Case Studies for Discernment

These fictional cases help readers apply the principles without treating them as formulas. For each case, identify the facts, the interests of both spouses, any moral or safety boundary, and a next step that can be reviewed. A facilitator should let participants reason before offering an interpretation.

**Case 1 — A job offer and a family obligation**

Daniel receives a job offer in another city. The salary is higher, but his wife Miriam has a stable position and is helping care for her widowed parent. Daniel believes the family should move and says that the passage requires Miriam to follow his decision. Miriam is not refusing the marriage; she is worried about her parent, her vocation, and the practical arrangements.

Begin by separating the decision from the theological argument. What is known about the job, the parent’s care needs, Miriam’s employment prospects, the cost of the move, and the timing? Are there alternatives such as delaying the move, commuting temporarily, arranging additional care, or declining the offer? Each spouse should be able to describe the other’s concern accurately. If the couple ultimately understands the husband to bear a particular decision responsibility, that does not remove his duty to weigh Miriam’s interests and own the consequences. The word “submit” should not replace practical wisdom.

**Case 2 — Spending shared savings**

Leila wants to use savings for an urgent roof repair. Mark wants to invest the same money in a business opportunity. Both options matter, but the couple does not yet know the repair deadline or the investment risk.

This is a stewardship question, not automatically a test of who is more submissive. Gather estimates, distinguish emergency expenses from opportunities, decide how much risk the household can bear, and agree on a decision date. If an immediate safety issue exists, protect the household first. A fair process includes transparent information and a record of what both spouses agreed to do.

**Case 3 — A repeated humiliation**

One spouse repeatedly makes jokes about the other’s body in front of friends. The harmed spouse says the jokes are humiliating and asks that they stop. The joking spouse replies that a respectful wife or husband should not be so sensitive.

The central issue is not whether the joke was intended as humor. The speaker has learned its effect and can stop. A repair begins by naming the behavior, acknowledging the harm, and changing it. The harmed spouse should not be required to laugh along to prove respect. If the conduct continues after clear requests, seek appropriate support and consider boundaries that protect dignity.

**Case 4 — Fear and monitoring**

A spouse says that their partner monitors communications, threatens consequences for disagreement, and has become physically violent. The person is afraid to discuss the relationship in a group or to let their partner know they sought help.

This is a safety concern, not an ordinary disagreement or a communication exercise. Do not ask the person to confront the partner, disclose details publicly, or make a quick decision about leaving. If danger is immediate, contact local emergency services. When possible, connect privately with a qualified local domestic-violence advocate who can help assess safe communication and next steps. A facilitator should respect confidentiality and understand its legal limits.

**Review questions**

1. What new information would change your understanding of each case?
2. Where does a spouse appeal to a biblical role to avoid listening or accountability?
3. Which cases call for a professional referral rather than a couple’s exercise?
4. What would a faithful, practical next step look like without promising an easy outcome?

## Chapter 17: Read the Words in Their Context

The interpretation of Ephesians 5 begins with the passage as a whole, not with one English word isolated from its sentences. Read Ephesians 5:15–33 and note the movement from wise, Spirit-filled life to the household instructions. The passage names singing, gratitude, reverence for Christ, submission, love, giving, cleansing, nourishment, cherishing, and one-flesh union. The repeated images work together; none should be used to cancel the others.

The word translated “as” introduces comparison or manner in these verses. It makes the comparison important, but it does not make the husband identical to Christ or the wife identical to the church in every respect. A comparison can set a standard without transferring every feature from one relationship to another. “As Christ loved” is a moral demand on the husband; it is not a claim that the husband saves, sanctifies, or possesses his wife.

The word translated “head” has generated discussion among Christian interpreters. Some emphasize authority and responsibility; others emphasize source, unity, or a combination of relational meanings. English translations cannot by themselves settle every interpretive question. Readers should consult the surrounding argument, compare responsible translations, and be honest about where a conclusion is an interpretation rather than a direct quotation.

The phrase “as unto the Lord” should be read with the rest of Christian teaching about allegiance and conscience. The wife’s obedience is offered to Christ; her husband is not placed in Christ’s position. The phrase does not bless a command that is sinful, abusive, or destructive. Likewise, “in everything” should be read as a comprehensive marital orientation, not as permission for coercive control.

Ephesians 5:21 is also important to interpretation. Christians differ on how its call to mutual submission relates grammatically and theologically to the following household instructions. Some traditions understand the text as preserving differentiated authority within a shared call to service; others emphasize reciprocal submission as the controlling pattern. A careful reader can explain a chosen view, acknowledge the existence of other readings, and still insist that every view must take Christlike love and the safety of persons seriously.

**Study and practice**

1. Read the passage in two translations and note both shared meaning and wording differences.
2. Label each conclusion as text, inference, or application.
3. Ask whether your interpretation gives equal attention to the commands addressed to wives and husbands.

## Chapter 18: Preparing for Covenant and Renewing It

The marriage passage is not only for couples in crisis. It can help people prepare for covenant and help long-married couples renew their practices. Preparation should address more than a wedding ceremony. It asks whether two people understand the promises they are making and have the skills and support to live them.

Before marriage, discuss faith and worship, expectations about roles, work and vocation, money and debt, children and fertility, household labor, sexual health and consent, relationships with extended family, conflict, and how each person responds to correction. Do not rely on hints or assume that a future spouse will change a deeply held belief after the wedding. Where expectations differ, name the difference and seek wise counsel before making a promise.

Pay attention to patterns, not only to assurances. Does each person tell the truth when it is costly? Can either person accept a boundary without retaliation? Is disagreement possible without threats, humiliation, or pressure? Does one person use spiritual authority to control the other’s choices or relationships? These questions do not guarantee a safe marriage, but they help identify concerns that deserve attention before a covenant is made.

Couples preparing for marriage can seek premarital counseling from a qualified pastor or counselor who takes both partners seriously. The counselor should allow private questions, explain confidentiality limits, and know when a specialist referral is necessary. A person should not be pressured to marry quickly to prove faith or commitment.

For married couples, renewal does not mean repeating vows as if nothing has happened. It can mean naming what has been strong, acknowledging harm, clarifying expectations, and making specific commitments for the next season. If there has been abuse or coercive control, a public vow renewal is not a substitute for safety, accountability, and qualified intervention.

**Covenant conversation**

Each spouse answers: What does faithfulness mean to me? What do I understand about my responsibilities? What am I afraid to say? What help would make this covenant safer and more sustainable? Listen without correcting the other person’s experience.

## Chapter 19: Household Stewardship, Money, and Unseen Work

Marriage becomes visible in the daily stewardship of time, money, energy, care, and attention. Ephesians does not provide a budget template or a chore chart. It does provide a moral test: does a spouse seek the other’s good, give truthfully, and treat the shared life as worthy of care?

Financial stewardship should be transparent enough that both spouses understand income, debt, obligations, savings, and major risks. A spouse should not use account access to punish or trap the other. Couples can agree on discretionary spending limits and joint approval thresholds, while still ensuring that both have practical access to necessary resources. When financial control or deprivation is occurring, the couple may need specialist support.

Unseen work matters. Remembering school dates, arranging medical appointments, buying groceries, tracking household supplies, caring for relatives, and noticing children’s emotional needs all take time and attention. If one spouse routinely performs this work, the other may benefit from it without seeing its cost. Naming the work is not scorekeeping; it is a first step toward a fair and sustainable arrangement.

Create a shared inventory of recurring tasks. Estimate how often each task occurs, who currently owns it, and whether the assignment fits each person’s time and ability. Agree on a trial adjustment and review it after a few weeks. The goal is not exact mathematical equality in every task; it is a household arrangement that both understand, can sustain, and can revise when circumstances change.

Children and caregiving should be part of the same conversation. Parents need consistency and cooperation, but neither spouse should use a child as a messenger, spy, or weapon. Decisions about discipline, schooling, health, and safety deserve shared information and age-appropriate attention to the child’s wellbeing.

**Study and practice**

1. List the household’s visible and invisible work.
2. Identify one task that could be transferred, shared, simplified, or supported by others.
3. Review the household budget together and agree on how major decisions will be made.

## Chapter 20: Forgiveness, Repentance, and Rebuilding Trust

Christian teaching values forgiveness, but forgiveness should not be confused with denial, instant trust, or unrestricted access. A spouse may forgive while still requiring changed behavior, firm boundaries, time, or professional help. No one should be pressured to announce forgiveness before they are ready or to use it as a reason to remain in danger.

Repentance begins with truthful ownership. The person who caused harm names what they did without shifting blame to stress, alcohol, a spouse’s behavior, or a misunderstanding. They listen to the impact, stop the behavior, accept reasonable consequences, and take steps that reduce the chance of repetition. If the behavior is serious or repeated, qualified treatment, pastoral accountability, or legal consequences may be appropriate.

Trust is rebuilt by evidence over time. Promises can begin the work but cannot complete it. The harmed spouse may need predictable communication, outside support, financial transparency, separate space, or a gradual return to responsibilities. Those needs should be discussed without retaliation. The person who caused harm cannot set the timeline for the other person’s recovery.

Reconciliation is relational and requires participation from both people. A spouse may choose not to reconcile, or may need separation, even while wishing the other person well. Legal, medical, pastoral, and safety questions should be addressed with qualified professionals. A theological account of marriage should not be presented as a guarantee that every relationship can or should continue in the same form.

For ordinary failures, couples can practice repair early: name the event, own a part, make a specific amends, and agree on a future action. Early repair keeps small hurts from hardening into a pattern. For abuse, this ordinary repair process is not enough; safety and specialist guidance come first.

**Study and practice**

1. What is the difference between forgiveness, trust, and reconciliation?
2. What observable actions would demonstrate change after a breach of trust?
3. Which concerns need help from a pastor, counselor, clinician, advocate, or attorney?
